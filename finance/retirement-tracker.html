A starting point, built 2026

You don't have a 401(k) yet. Here's the whole picture.

Three things, in order: figure out which account you can actually open, see what contributing does over time, then track what you put in. Nothing here needs an account or sends your numbers anywhere — everything you type stays in this browser.

First

Which door are you walking through?

"I don't have a 401(k)" means different things, and the fix is different for each. Find the row that matches you — that's your next action.

Most common

My job offers one, I never enrolled

Very common — many plans need you to opt in, and the paperwork is easy to ignore. Every pay period you skip is match money you don't get back.

Do this: Open your benefits portal (or email HR) and ask: do we have a 401(k), what's the match, and when can I enroll? Set your contribution to at least the full match.

No plan at work

My employer doesn't offer one

You can't open a 401(k) on your own — it's an employer plan. The individual equivalent is an IRA, which you open yourself at any brokerage in about 15 minutes.

Do this: Open a Roth IRA at a major low-cost brokerage. 2026 limit is $7,500. Set an automatic monthly transfer, then pick an investment — money sitting in the account uninvested doesn't grow.

Self-employed

I'm 1099, freelance, or a business owner

You get the best options of anyone. A Solo 401(k) lets you contribute as both employee and employer, so the ceiling is far higher than a regular plan.

Do this: Compare a Solo 401(k) against a SEP-IRA. Both are opened at a brokerage; the Solo 401(k) usually allows more at lower incomes and permits Roth contributions.

Waiting period

I'm new and not eligible yet

Plans can make you wait — often up to a year of service. That's a delay, not a dead end, and it doesn't stop you saving elsewhere meanwhile.

Do this: Ask HR for your exact eligibility date and put it in your calendar. Fund an IRA in the meantime; you can do both in the same year.

Second

The order to do things in

Money you invest can only be spent once, so sequence matters more than picking the perfect fund. This order is deliberately boring and it's roughly what most fee-only planners will tell you.

  1. A small cash buffer

    One month of expenses in plain savings, before investing anything. Without it, the first surprise bill turns into credit-card debt or an early withdrawal with a penalty.

  2. The full employer match

    If your job matches contributions, contribute at least enough to get all of it. A 50% match is an immediate 50% return before the market does anything at all.

    Nothing else you can buy pays this well. Do it even while carrying debt.

  3. High-interest debt

    Anything above roughly 7–8% — credit cards especially. Paying off a 22% card is a guaranteed 22% return; no investment offers that with certainty.

  4. Top the emergency fund to 3–6 months

    This is what keeps the invested money invested through a job loss. Keep it in a high-yield savings account, not in the market.

  5. Max an IRA

    $7,500 for 2026. You choose the brokerage and the investments, and the fees are usually lower than a workplace plan's menu. Roth if you expect your tax rate to be higher later, which is typical early in a career.

  6. Back to the 401(k), up to the max

    $24,500 of your own salary in 2026. Most people never reach this step, and that's fine — it's a ceiling, not a target.

  7. A regular taxable brokerage account

    No contribution limit and no early-withdrawal penalty, which makes it the right home for goals before retirement age.

Plain language

The words people use without explaining them

401(k)
A retirement account your employer sets up. Money moves straight from your paycheck, usually before tax is taken, and it's yours if you leave — you take it with you.
IRA
The same idea, but you open it yourself at a brokerage. Lower limit than a 401(k), no employer match, more control over what you invest in.
Traditional vs. Roth
Traditional: skip tax now, pay it when you withdraw. Roth: pay tax now, withdraw completely tax-free later. Roth usually wins when you're early in your career and your tax rate is lower than it will be.
Employer match
Your employer adds money when you do — often 50% or 100% of your contribution, up to a few percent of pay. Not contributing enough to get all of it means turning down salary.
Vesting
How long you must stay before the employer's contributions are permanently yours. Your own contributions are always yours immediately. Check the schedule before you resign.
Index fund
A fund that buys the whole market instead of trying to beat it. Cheap, boring, and it outperforms most managed funds over long periods.
Target-date fund
A single fund named for the year you'll retire ("Target 2060"). It holds a mix of stocks and bonds and gets more conservative as that date approaches. A reasonable one-choice answer if you don't want to think about it.
Expense ratio
The annual fee a fund takes, as a percentage. 0.03% is excellent; above 0.50% deserves a hard look. Small differences compound into large ones over thirty years.
Rollover
Moving an old employer's 401(k) into an IRA or a new plan when you change jobs. Done correctly there's no tax and no penalty — this is how you stop losing track of old accounts.
RMD
Required minimum distribution — the age at which the government makes you start withdrawing from pre-tax accounts. Roth IRAs are exempt for the original owner.

This is educational, not financial advice. It's a calculator and a notebook — it doesn't know your tax situation, debts, or plans. The projections are arithmetic on the assumptions you type in, not predictions: real markets deliver their average through years that are nothing like average, and a long decline early in retirement hurts far more than the same decline late.
